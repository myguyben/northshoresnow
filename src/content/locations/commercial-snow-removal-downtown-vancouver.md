## Downtown's hazard arrives without a storm

Downtown Vancouver gets less snow than anywhere else we serve and generates a comparable
amount of ice, for reasons that have nothing to do with snowfall.

Harbour wind moves damp air through tower canyons that never see sun in December. Shaded
frontages stay wet all day and then radiate heat away on a clear night. Meltwater from a
warmer surface runs onto a colder one and stops. By 6am the granite in front of an office
tower is glazed and the forecast said nothing about precipitation.

A contract that dispatches on accumulation does not send anyone on those nights. That is why
de-icing here runs on a temperature trigger and is priced as its own visit — in a downtown
winter, most billable visits are ice control.

## Polished stone behaves differently

This is a small, specific thing that downtown buildings get caught by repeatedly.

Office entrances downtown are granite, polished concrete or stone tile rather than the
broom-finished sidewalk in front of them. Those surfaces hold a thinner, more even film of
water and release heat faster. The result is that a building's own entrance is frequently
the slickest surface on the block while the public sidewalk two metres away is merely wet —
and it is the surface with a door, a revolving entry and a queue of people coming through it.

Entrance materials are part of how we plan treatment, including which de-icer goes where.
Some products damage stone and coloured concrete, which is worth knowing before a season
rather than after a building's frontage is pitted.

## The bay apron cascades

Downtown loading is booked. Trucks arrive in narrow scheduled windows, often with no
flexibility to come back later, because the next booking is already assigned.

An icy or snow-blocked bay apron therefore does not delay one delivery. It pushes the whole
day's schedule and, in a shared bay, other tenants' schedules with it. The apron is mapped
as its own priority surface and sequenced against your delivery windows rather than cleared
in whatever order the route happens to reach it.

The same logic applies to the parkade ramp, and specifically to its lip. A heated garage
sends meltwater down the ramp to the coldest point — the join with the street — where it
refreezes across the full width of the only vehicle access the building has. The ramp and
its lip are serviced as one surface.

## Two or three frontages, cleared by 10am

The City requires property owners to clear the sidewalks bordering their property **by 10am
after a snowfall**. A corner building owns two; a through-block site can own three.

Downtown frontages are long, continuously walked and highly visible, and they are the most
likely place for a business to be photographed or reported. Count yours before comparing
quotes — a bidder pricing one frontage against a competitor pricing three looks cheaper and
is not.

## What separates contractors downtown

- **Does de-icing dispatch on temperature alone?** If not, the contract will be idle through
  the mornings that matter.
- **Is the ramp lip in scope, or does the map stop at the property line?**
- **Is the bay apron sequenced to delivery windows?**
- **Where does snow go, and is removal priced now?** Downtown sites usually have no on-site
  storage at all.
- **What is the response window?** Ours is in the agreement; downtown runs on extended storm
  dispatch against the same weather triggers as our core routes.
